### Explanation

**Subquestion 1**
* **Blank A:** Table 2 shows the mapping of inside local addresses to the router's NAT addresses. A response sent from the SaaS server to `198.51.100.20:3312` is mapped back by the NAT router. Looking at Table 2, the source `192.168.1.6:4524` was translated to `198.51.100.20:3312`. Therefore, the response packet will be delivered to `192.168.1.6`. (Correct Answer: **b**)
* **Blank B:** Computer Lab 2 has 20 PCs for trainees, 1 PC for the trainer, 1 file server, 1 printer, and 1 router interface, totaling 24 devices. This requires at least 24 assignable IP addresses. 
  - A `/28` subnet only provides 14 assignable IPs.
  - A `/27` subnet provides $2^5 - 2 = 30$ assignable IPs, which is sufficient. 
  A `/27` subnet mask corresponds to `255.255.255.224`. (Correct Answer: **d**)
* **Blank C:** The first subnet for Lab 1 was `192.168.1.0/28` (ranging from `.0` to `.15`). The next available block must be aligned to a `/27` boundary (multiples of 32). Thus, the new subnet begins at `192.168.1.32` and ends at `192.168.1.63`. (Correct Answer: **c**)
* **Blank D:** For the subnet `192.168.1.32/27`, the network address is `.32` and the broadcast address is `.63`. The "last assignable IP address" is the address right before the broadcast address, which is `192.168.1.62`. (Correct Answer: **c**)

---

**Subquestion 2**
* **Blank E:** The devices were upgraded to support 10 Gbps and 1 Gbps interfaces. However, the physical infrastructure is still using **UTP Cat-5 cables**. 
  Category 5 (Cat-5) cables reliably support up to 100 Mbps. While they can sometimes support 1 Gbps (1000BASE-T) over short distances, they are completely incapable of supporting **10 Gbps** links (which connect the switches to the routers). To utilize the new 10 Gbps bandwidth, the physical cabling must be upgraded to **Cat-6a** (or better), which is rated for 10 Gbps over standard distances. (Correct Answer: **a**)
