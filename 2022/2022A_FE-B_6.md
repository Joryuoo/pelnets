### Explanation

#### Subquestion 1
**Blank A:**
Step 1 aims to count the occurrences of each exam score. We iterate over the 10 students (`i` from 0 to 9), retrieve their score `Score[i]`, and increment the corresponding tally in the `Count` array. Therefore, `A` is `Count[Score[i]]`.

**Blank B and C:**
Step 2 calculates the starting output index (`Index`) for each possible score. The running total `n` accumulates the number of elements processed so far.
For a given score `i` (from 0 to 10), its starting position in the sorted array is exactly `n` (the total number of elements with scores lower than `i`). Thus, **B** is `n`. 
After assigning `Index[i] = n`, we must increment `n` by the number of students who got score `i` before moving to score `i+1`. Thus, we add `Count[i]` to `n`. So, **C** is `Count[i]`.

**Blank D and E:**
Step 3 populates the output arrays. We loop through the original students (`i` from 0 to 9). For student `i`, their score is `Score[i]`. The position where they should be placed is retrieved from `Index[Score[i]]`. Thus, `n = Index[Score[i]]`, making **D** `Index[Score[i]]`.
Once placed, if another student has the exact same score, they must be placed in the *next* adjacent slot. Therefore, we must increment the starting position for that score by 1: `Index[Score[i]] = Index[Score[i]] + 1`. Thus, **E** is `Index[Score[i]]`.

**Blank F:**
Counting sort iterates through the input data of size $n$ a constant number of times (Step 1 and Step 3 take $O(n)$ time, Step 2 takes $O(k)$ where $k$ is the max score range which is constant here). Overall, the calculation performance scales linearly with the number of input items, so it is $O(n)$. Thus, **F** is $O(n)$.

#### Subquestion 2
**Blank G:**
To sort in descending order using Method 2, we must map the ascending positions to descending positions. Since there are 10 elements, valid output indices are 0 through 9. A score that would have been placed at position `n` in an ascending array must now be placed at the mirrored position from the end, which is `9 - n`. Thus, **G** is `9 - n`.

**Blank H:**
- **Method 1 Stability:** Method 1 changes Step 2 to iterate backwards (`i` from 10 down to 0). Higher scores are assigned lower starting indices. In Step 3, students are processed from left to right (0 to 9). The first student encountered with a score $S$ is placed at `Index[S]`, and `Index[S]` is incremented. The next student with score $S$ will be placed at `Index[S] + 1` (to the right). Their relative order is preserved, so **Method 1 is stable**.
- **Method 2 Stability:** Method 2 keeps Step 2 ascending. In Step 3, the first student with score $S$ gets index $n$, and is placed at $9 - n$. `Index[S]` is incremented to $n+1$. The next student with score $S$ gets index $n+1$, and is placed at $9 - (n+1) = 8 - n$. Since $8 - n$ is *before* $9 - n$, the second student is placed to the left of the first student, reversing their original order. Thus, **Method 2 is unstable**.

| Method | Loop Direction (Step 2) | Array Output Mapping (Step 3) | Relative Order | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Method 1** | Descending (`i=10` to `0`) | Ascending (`0` to `9`) | Maintains Left-to-Right | **Stable** |
| **Method 2** | Ascending (`i=0` to `10`) | Mirrored (`9-n`) | Reverses Left-to-Right | **Unstable** |

Therefore, **H** is method 1 is stable and method 2 is unstable.
